Software Process Models

Software process models define what specific activities are included in a software process and in what order they are included.

Different software projects may require different process models depending on requirements, risks, customer involvement, testing needs, and the nature of the project.

Model 1

1. Waterfall Model

The Waterfall Model is a linear sequential flow, in which progress is seen as flowing steadily downwards (like a waterfall) through the phases of software implementation.

This means that any phase in the development process begins only if the previous phase is complete.

The waterfall approach does not define the process to go back to the previous phase to handle changes in requirements.

The waterfall approach is the earliest approach and one of the most widely known approaches used for software development.

Type: Plan-driven model

Advantages

  • Easy to explain to the users.
  • Stages are well defined.
  • Helps to plan and schedule the project.
  • Each phase has specific deliverables.

Disadvantages

  • Assumes that the requirements of a system can be frozen.
  • Very difficult to go back to any stage after it is finished.
  • Little flexibility; adjusting the scope is difficult and expensive.
  • Costly and requires more time because of the detailed plan.
Model 2

2. V-Shaped Model

The V-Shaped Model is an extension of the Waterfall Model.

Instead of moving down in a linear way, the process steps are bent upwards after the implementation and coding phase, forming the typical V shape.

Major difference from Waterfall: Early test planning in the V-shaped model.

↘

←

Insert V-Shaped Model illustration here

Advantages

  • Simple and easy to use.
  • Each phase has specific deliverables.
  • Higher chance of success over the Waterfall Model due to early development of test plans.
  • Works well where requirements are easily understood.
  • Verification and validation of the product are considered in the early stages of product development.

Disadvantages

  • Very inflexible, like the Waterfall Model.
  • Adjusting the scope is difficult and expensive.
  • The software is developed during the implementation phase, so no early prototypes of the software are produced.
  • The model does not provide a clear path for problems found during the testing phases.
  • Costly and requires more time and a detailed plan.
Model 3

3. Prototyping Model

The Prototyping Model refers to the activity of creating prototypes of software applications, for example, incomplete versions of the software program being developed.

A prototype is used to visualize some components of the software and to limit the gap of misunderstanding customer requirements by the development team.

This can reduce the iterations that may occur in the Waterfall approach and that are difficult to implement because of its inflexibility.

When the final requirements have been agreed upon, the requirements are considered frozen before final development.

3.1 Throwaway Prototyping

Prototypes that are eventually discarded rather than becoming part of the finally delivered software.

  • It can be costly to implement prototypes.
Model 4

4. Spiral Model

The Spiral Model combines iterative development with risk analysis.

Key idea: Emphasis on risk reduction at every step.

This model is evolutionary. The system grows in increments, but each cycle focuses on evaluating and mitigating risks.

The process is represented as a spiral rather than as a sequence of activities with backtracking.

Each loop in the spiral represents a phase or cycle in the process.

There are no fixed phases such as specification or design. The activities in each loop are chosen depending on what is required.

Risks are explicitly assessed and resolved throughout the process.

Advantages

  • Estimates such as budget and schedule become more realistic as work progresses because important issues are discovered earlier.
  • Early involvement of developers.
  • Manages risks and develops the system into phases.

Disadvantages

  • High cost and time to reach the final product.
  • Needs special skills to evaluate risks and assumptions.
  • Highly customized, limiting reusability.
Model 5

5. Iterative and Incremental Model

The Iterative and Incremental Model was developed to overcome some of the weaknesses of the Waterfall Model.

It starts with initial planning and ends with deployment, with cyclic interactions in between.

The basic idea is to develop a system through repeated cycles (iterative) and in smaller portions at a time (incremental).

This allows software developers to take advantage of what was learned during the development of earlier parts or versions of the system.

It can consist of mini-Waterfalls or mini V-Shaped models.

Remember:
Incremental = adding functionality piece by piece.
Iterative = repeatedly improving/refining the system.

Advantages

  • Produces business value early in the development lifecycle.
  • Better use of scarce resources through proper increment definition.
  • Can accommodate some change requests between increments.
  • More focused on customer value than linear approaches.
  • Project issues and changes can be detected earlier.

Disadvantages

  • Integration between iterations can be an issue if it is not considered during development and project planning.

Quick Comparison of Process Models

Model Main Idea Best When
Waterfall Linear sequential development Requirements are stable and well understood
V-Model Development phases matched with testing phases Verification and validation are very important
Prototyping Build prototypes to understand requirements Requirements are unclear or difficult to understand
Spiral Iterative development with explicit risk analysis Projects have significant risks
Iterative & Incremental Develop and refine the system in small cycles Early delivery, feedback, and gradual development are needed

Exam Memory Guide

  • Waterfall → Fixed/stable requirements
  • V-Model → Testing and validation
  • Prototyping → Unclear requirements
  • Spiral → Risk analysis
  • Incremental → Add functionality in pieces
  • Iterative → Refine and improve repeatedly
