PART 01 · THE PROBLEM

Why MinHash alone isn't enough

MinHash compresses documents into small signatures while preserving their expected Jaccard similarity. But finding the most similar pairs can still be expensive.

The quadratic problem

Suppose we have \(n\) documents. The number of possible document pairs is:

\[ \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]

Even a relatively modest collection can produce an enormous number of pairs.

The goal of LSH

We don't want to compare every possible pair. Instead, we want to identify pairs that are likely to be similar .

Key idea
Hash similar objects into the same buckets with high probability, while making collisions between dissimilar objects unlikely.

PART 02 · LOCALITY-SENSITIVE HASHING

What makes a hash function "locality-sensitive"?

A locality-sensitive hash function is designed so that similar objects are more likely to collide than dissimilar objects.

\[ \text{similar}(x,y) \Rightarrow P[h(x)=h(y)] \text{ is high} \]
\[ \text{dissimilar}(x,y) \Rightarrow P[h(x)=h(y)] \text{ is low} \]

Ordinary hashing

A conventional hash function tries to distribute inputs uniformly. Similar inputs do not need to remain close.

Locality-sensitive hashing

LSH deliberately makes the collision probability depend on similarity.

Similarity becomes a signal for collision probability.

PART 03 · MINHASH

MinHash is locality-sensitive for Jaccard similarity

MinHash gives us a compact signature while preserving an important property of set similarity.

The key property

\[ P[h(A)=h(B)] = J(A,B) \]

The probability that two sets receive the same MinHash value is exactly their Jaccard similarity.

PART 05 · BANDS & BUCKETS

How LSH creates candidate pairs

Each signature is divided into bands. Each band is hashed independently into a bucket.

The banding strategy

\[ \boxed{ b=\text{number of bands} } \qquad \boxed{ r=\text{rows per band} } \]

With 12 signature rows, one possible configuration is:

\[ 12 = 3 \times 4 \]
3 bands × 4 rows per band

PART 06 · PROBABILITY

Why the banding strategy works

The magic of LSH comes from the probability that a pair agrees completely inside at least one band.

Step 1 — One band

Suppose two documents have similarity \(s\). The probability that they agree in all \(r\) rows of one band is:

\[ s^r \]

Step 2 — One band fails

Therefore, the probability that they disagree in at least one row of the band is:

\[ 1-s^r \]

Step 3 — All bands fail

If there are \(b\) independent bands, the probability that every band fails is:

\[ (1-s^r)^b \]

Step 4 — Candidate probability

Therefore, the probability that the pair agrees in all rows of at least one band is:

\[ \boxed{ 1-(1-s^r)^b } \]
Important
LSH does not say that similar documents are guaranteed to collide. It changes the probability of collision so that highly similar pairs are much more likely to become candidates.

PART 07 · THE S-CURVE

The probability transition

The candidate probability \(1-(1-s^r)^b\) produces the characteristic S-shaped curve.

3
4
Approximate threshold: 0.760

Approximate threshold

\[ \boxed{ t \approx \left(\frac{1}{b}\right)^{1/r} } \]

Around this similarity level, the candidate probability rises rapidly. Increasing the number of bands generally makes more pairs candidates, while increasing the number of rows per band makes collisions more selective.

PART 08 · INTUITION

Why "any band" matters

Imagine two highly similar documents. Their MinHash signatures will agree frequently.

Dividing the signature into bands gives them many opportunities to match completely within one band.

\[ \text{High similarity} \Rightarrow \text{high chance of a complete band match} \]

Meanwhile, a dissimilar pair is unlikely to match in all rows of even one band.

\[ \text{Low similarity} \Rightarrow \text{low chance of becoming a candidate} \]

MinHash

Compresses sets into signatures while preserving expected Jaccard similarity.

Bands

Create multiple independent opportunities for similar signatures to match.

Buckets

Turn matching bands into candidate pairs that deserve further comparison.

FINAL TAKEAWAY

From millions of pairs to a small candidate set

\[ \text{Documents} \rightarrow \text{Shingles} \rightarrow \text{MinHash signatures} \rightarrow \text{Bands} \rightarrow \text{Buckets} \rightarrow \text{Candidate pairs} \]
1 · MinHash
Preserves Jaccard similarity probabilistically.
2 · LSH
Focuses computation on likely similar pairs.
3 · Candidate pairs
Only promising pairs need expensive comparison.
Remember the core equation:
\[ \boxed{ P(\text{candidate}) = 1-(1-s^r)^b } \]
Similarity \(s\), bands \(b\), and rows per band \(r\) together determine the probability that two documents become an LSH candidate pair.
